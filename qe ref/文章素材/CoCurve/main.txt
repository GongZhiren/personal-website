                                            A BSTRACT
         Structured pruning removes whole attention heads and feed-forward channel
         groups, but training-free methods rank those units independently, as if the dam-
         age from removing a set were the sum of the damage from removing each one.
         Transformer sublayers are coupled through a shared residual stream, so two units
         that look individually weak can be jointly indispensable, and independent scoring
         deletes them together. We introduce CoCurve (Cross-Module Co-Pruning Curva-
         ture), which prunes attention and FFN units under one shared budget. Expanding
         the token-level KL between the frozen model and its masked copy to second order
         yields a single Fisher matrix whose diagonal is classical node saliency and whose
         off-diagonal is the co-pruning curvature between units. That matrix is a Gram
         product of single-unit ablation features, so it follows from M ablations rather
         than O(M 2 ) pairwise sweeps — no gradients, no weight update — and pruning
         becomes one budgeted quadratic program solved in a single shot. How far to trust
         the off-diagonal is measured rather than tuned: λ indexes a finite family of prun-
         ings whose breakpoints we enumerate exactly, and we keep the member with the
         lowest held-out calibration risk, using no labels and no benchmarks. Across five
         models from 3B to 24B at three ratios, CoCurve leads on 38 of 45 perplexity cells
         against a row holding, in each cell, whichever of seven training-free baselines
         wins it, and on 666 of 721 cells against those baselines one at a time; the strongest
         of them backpropagates through the whole model and so needs 94 GB to select at
         24B, more than an 80 GB card has. Read along the other axis, at equal perplexity
         CoCurve removes 2.2 to 6.6 more points of the network than the strongest baseline
         at ρ=30–40%. The off-diagonal also acts on a population we can name and delete
         on demand: forcing out the low-saliency, high-connectivity bridge units against
         a cost- and type-matched control costs nineteen of twenty capability-group cells
         across five models, while on three of the five perplexity moves the other way.
         Nothing in the construction is specific to a decoder-only stack: it transfers un-
         changed to seven vision–language models under one budget shared across both
         towers, leading every training-free baseline on five of them at ρ=20% with no
         baseline ahead of it by as much as a point on any. CoCurve reframes structured
         LLM pruning: prune the edges, not just the nodes.


1   I NTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) deliver their capabilities at a parameter and latency cost that is
prohibitive for most deployments (Vaswani et al., 2017; Dubey et al., 2024). Structured pruning
removes whole computational units, such as attention heads and feed-forward (FFN) channel groups.
It is the most deployment-friendly route to compression: the pruned model stays dense and runs
faster on commodity hardware, without the specialized kernels that unstructured or semi-structured
sparsity needs (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023; Sun et al., 2024). The most practical setting is training-
free, pruning one-shot from a small unlabeled calibration set, which avoids the cost and the data
dependence of retraining (Ma et al., 2023; An et al., 2024).
The blind spot: nodes without edges. Structured pruners score each removable unit and delete the
lowest-ranked ones, using weight (Han et al., 2015), activation (Sun et al., 2024; An et al., 2024)
or local loss statistics (Ma et al.,
                                 ∑︁2023; Frantar & Alistarh, 2022). Ranking assumes that set-level
damage is additive, ∆L(S) ≈ u∈S ∆L(u). In a Transformer it is not. Attention and FFN units

read and write one shared residual stream, so pruning is a joint operation on a coupled system: two
units that look weak alone can be jointly indispensable, and two salient-looking ones can be partly
redundant when their perturbations overlap. A ranking is blind to this even when attention and FFN
units share a single global order, and it will delete a low-saliency bridge unit together with the units
that depend on it. The interaction has to be modeled. Prune the edges, not just the nodes.
One matrix, read two ways. Interactions could be added heuristically — by correlating activations,
say — but that measures the wrong thing, and not by a little. Activation correlation is a property of
a layer’s inputs; pruning dependence is a property of how two removals jointly move the model’s
output. Nothing in the former refers to the output at all, and two perfectly correlated units may be
jointly harmless, because each covers for the other, or jointly critical, because whatever consumes
them needs both — the correlation is identical in the two cases. An interaction term worth having
must therefore be derived from the quantity being protected, so we derive it from the pruning objec-
tive. Define pruning risk as the token-level KL divergence between the frozen model and its masked
copy over unlabeled text. The masked model equals the frozen one at zero pruning, so both the
value and the gradient of this risk vanish there, and the local expansion begins at second order as a
quadratic form 21 s⊤ Hs in the structured mask s. That single matrix has a direct reading (Figure 1):

its diagonal Huu is classical node saliency; its off-diagonal Huv is a co-pruning curvature edge,
      the mixed curvature governing the predicted marginal risk of removing u and v together.

These are not hand-designed information-flow arrows but symmetric interactions in the same Fisher
geometry that defines the saliency, spanning attention↔FFN pairs across all layers. Pruning be-
comes global co-pruning-risk minimisation rather than node ranking, and it inherits a classical floor:
dropping the off-diagonal recovers an Optimal-Brain-Damage saliency (LeCun et al., 1989; Hassibi
& Stork, 1993) lifted to structured units, so CoCurve is its cross-module generalisation.
Why the off-diagonal is affordable. Ablate each removable unit once and record how the output
distribution moves. The inner product of two such recorded shifts is exactly the entry of H for that
pair, so the full M × M matrix is a Gram product of M ablations rather than O(M 2 ) pairwise
sweeps, and no gradient is ever taken. Units are then removed greedily under one shared budget,
each candidate charged its own cost plus its accumulated interaction with what has already gone, so
the attention-versus-FFN split follows from the objective instead of being fixed by hand. Ablations
replace the derivative columns, and Section B.2 measures what that substitution costs. Nothing is
trained. The one quantity that must be chosen, how far to trust the pairwise term, is measured rather
than tuned: it indexes a family of prunings that is finite and whose breakpoints we enumerate exactly,
in the spirit of LARS (Efron et al., 2004), and we keep the member with the lowest held-out risk,
consulting no labels and no benchmarks.

What this buys. We evaluate five dense LLMs from 3B to 24B across the Llama-3, Falcon3 and
Mistral lineages against seven training-free baselines, under one shared guard and harness, on three
language-modelling corpora and twelve multiple-choice benchmarks scored on their full test sets
at five ratios from 10% to 50%, all five of them on Mistral-Small-24B. Against the per-column
best of every baseline — an envelope, not one competitor — CoCurve takes 70 of the 105 cells
this grid contains: 38 of the 45 perplexity cells and 32 of the 60 capability-group cells. Nothing
in the construction is specific to a decoder-only stack: pointed at seven vision–language models,
where the unit inventory spans two towers and one budget is shared across both, the same estimator
and the same selector lead every training-free baseline on all five headline columns (Section 3.6).
The edge term also acts through a population we can name and delete on demand. Low-saliency,
high-connectivity bridge units are what node-first scoring removes first; forcing them out, against a
control matched unit-for-unit on saliency, unit type and cost and completed by the same shared fill,
costs nineteen of twenty capability-group cells across five models, by up to 23.7 points — while on
three of the five perplexity moves the other way. Given one budget and no per-type rule, the same
objective independently arrives at the same allocation across five models and five ratios: the first half
of a network’s depth pruned entirely out of FFN width, attention touched only beyond it, in thirteen
of the seventeen solutions that remove any attention at all — and eight of the twenty-five solutions,
on two of the models, remove none anywhere.

2     M ETHOD
We target dense decoder-only LLMs and prune two kinds of structured units: attention units (a
head in multi-head attention, or a KV-aligned query-head group in grouped-query attention (Ainslie
et al., 2023)) and FFN groups (contiguous blocks of intermediate channels). Let U = {u1 , . . . , uM }
collect all such units across all layers, with a removable parameter cost cu = Param(u) each. Each
unit carries a keep gate mu scaling its write into the residual stream, and we write su = 1 − mu
for the pruning variable: su =0 keeps unit u and su =1 zeroes its write. The deployed mask is
binary, s ∈ {0, 1}M . The gate is real-valued, so R below is defined on a neighbourhood of 0 in
RM and 0 is an interior point rather than a vertex of the cube, which is what makes its expansion
well posed. Speedups are measured on models whose weight matrices are physically sliced, not on
masked full-size ones (Section A.2).

2.4   I NTERACTION STRENGTH : A PATH , NOT A HYPERPARAMETER

The diagonal follows from single-unit ablations alone. The off-diagonal additionally assumes that
simultaneous removals perturb the logits additively (Eq. 5), so we weight it by a single interaction
strength λ ∈ [0, 1],
                            Rˆ︁ λ (s) = 1 ∑︁ Huu su + λ ∑︁

so that λ = 0 recovers pure node saliency and λ = 1 the full edge model. The λ = 0 endpoint is a floor
rather than an arbitrary limit. It is exactly the OBD diagonal selector (Section B.3), so whatever λ
we end up with, the method degrades at worst to a strong classical second-order baseline.
Choosing it is not a fitting problem. The solver consumes only the order the marginals induce, so λ
has to be chosen by what the selections it produces actually cost — and that quantity turns out to be
exactly computable.

so within a single greedy step the arg-min can change only where two of these lines cross. Every

The family can be enumerated exactly. Because Eq. 10 is affine, the crossings are available in
closed form. Starting at λ = 0, the earliest crossing over all greedy steps is the right endpoint of
the interval on which the current solution is optimal. Stepping just past it and re-solving moves to
the next member, and repeating walks the whole family; the step past each crossing is 10−9 , nearly
three orders of magnitude below the narrowest interval on which any member of the ten enumerated
families is optimal, 7.6 × 10−7 (Section B.3.1). The construction is the same critical-value walk that
makes the LASSO path enumerable by LARS (Efron et al., 2004), borrowed for its mechanics rather
than its guarantees: LARS traces the exact optimum of a convex problem and its path is piecewise
linear, while Sλ is the path of a greedy heuristic and is piecewise constant. The walk is CPU-only,
one greedy solve per breakpoint and no forward passes, and what the selection then costs is one
masked forward pass per distinct member — 303 of them on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Sampling λ is
not a substitute at any price. The median interval width is 1.4 × 10−3 across the ten models, so a
grid at step 0.002 still reaches a median of only 61% of the members and as few as 35%: it misses
solutions rather than costing more (Section B.3.1).
Selection is then a held-out measurement. With the family in hand there is nothing left to search
over; we keep the member with the lowest measured held-out risk,
                                     λ̂ = arg min R(Sλ ; C ′ ),                                    (11)

where R(· ; C ′ ) is the same token-level KL of Eq. 1, measured on a calibration split C ′ held out from
the one that built H. No labels and no benchmark enter, and each Sλ is judged by measured risk
rather than by the surrogate that proposed it. Every member is scored on the same sequences, so
differences across λ are paired and we report them in paired standard errors. The cost is one masked
forward pass per member over a small held-out set, against the M ablations H already requires.
Two properties of this rule matter for how the results below should be read. First, the setting is
fixed before any benchmark is seen: λ̂ is chosen on held-out risk, so no test set enters the selection,
and we deliberately do not pick the member that scores best downstream — a family member tuned
on perplexity would be a different and much weaker claim. Second, the criterion is flat near its
optimum. Neighbouring members sit within its own noise: on the headline model the member
at λ=0.15 carries 2.1% more held-out risk, 1.3 paired standard errors, and lands at a comparable
operating point. The rule therefore does not need to be delicate to be useful, and Section D.11
confirms that resampling the held-out set and changing the selection protocol move it very little.

3     E XPERIMENTS
3.1   S ETUP

Models. Five dense decoder-only LLMs spanning the Llama-3, Falcon3 and Mistral lineages and
the 3B–24B range: Llama-3.2-3B (Dubey et al., 2024), Falcon3-7B (Falcon-LLM Team, 2024),
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Dubey et al., 2024), Mistral-Nemo-12B and Mistral-Small-24B (Jiang
et al., 2023). Four are base pretrained checkpoints and one is instruction-tuned, so the comparison
spans both regimes; all use grouped-query attention, so attention units are KV-aligned query-head
groups.
Benchmarks. We follow the evaluation protocol established for training-free structured prun-
ing (Ma et al., 2023; An et al., 2024; Ling et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025): language-modelling
perplexity together with zero-shot multiple-choice accuracy. Perplexity is measured on WikiText-
2 (Merity et al., 2017), PTB (Marcus et al., 1993) and C4 (Raffel et al., 2020) at a 2048-token con-
text. Accuracy is measured on twelve benchmarks covering commonsense reasoning (PIQA (Bisk
et al., 2020), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), Common-
senseQA (Talmor et al., 2019)), science question answering (ARC-easy/challenge (Clark et al.,
2018), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018)), reading comprehension (BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019),
RACE and RACE-high (Lai et al., 2017), QuAIL (Rogers et al., 2020)) and world knowledge
(MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021)). Every task is scored on its full test set through one shared
harness (Section C.2).
We report both families, and it is worth saying at the outset which one carries which claim. Perplex-
ity is how this literature compares methods, and we report it so our numbers are readable against
published ones; it is also the family our objective is closest to. But a language-modelling loss can
miss capability damage — Section 4 exhibits a mask that improves WikiText while losing double
digits on every capability group — so we never let a perplexity win stand in for a capability claim.
Where the two families disagree we say so and let the accuracy columns decide. The result is that
the lead does not rest on the weaker witness: it is present in both families at once.
Baselines. Seven training-free baselines under identical budgets and harness: Random, Magni-
tude (Han et al., 2015), Wanda-sp (Sun et al., 2024; An et al., 2024), FLAP (An et al., 2024),
LLM-Pruner (Ma et al., 2023), SlimGPT (Ling et al., 2024) and SlimLLM (Guo et al., 2025).
Section C.3 adds two more on the headline model: AMP (Mugnaini et al., 2025) inside the same
guard, and the two-stage 2SSP (Sandri et al., 2025) under its own per-layer budget, because its
two stages are the method. The regime is selection-only: the question is which units to remove,
so post-pruning weight repair and fine-tuning are disabled for every method including ours, namely
LLM-Pruner’s LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), SlimLLM’s regression update, SlimGPT’s OBS update and
FLAP’s bias compensation. Section 3.5 adds an identical LoRA stage to every selection at once.

3.2    M AIN RESULTS

The gap opens as compression bites. Table 1 reports the headline model task by task. Across the
three budgets it is first or second in all forty-five columns, and first in twenty-seven of them; the
best competitor reaches thirteen of fifteen in its strongest block and no other method exceeds five.
At 30% CoCurve holds ten of the fifteen columns outright — all three perplexities and seven of
the twelve accuracy tasks, including world knowledge and both science benchmarks. Our WikiText
margin over the best baseline widens monotonically with the budget on this model, from 2.6% at
20% to 19.0% at 30% and 42.8% at 40%. A coupling account predicts exactly this. While a model is
over-parameterised, almost any 20% of its units can go without the survivors having to compensate.
Once the budget forces removals that genuinely depend on one another, the term that models that
dependence decides whether the remaining network still computes anything. Nothing about the
method changes across the sweep: the same objective, estimator and solver, with λ̂ re-derived at
each ratio by Eq. 11 rather than scheduled. On Falcon3-7B it returns 0.218 at 20% and 0.149 at 30%
without ever being shown the ratio.

a strict bar: the per-column best of every baseline run for it — a bar that in ten of the fifteen blocks
no single competitor attains, and that in the other five LLM-Pruner attains alone. The envelope
carries all seven baselines on thirteen of the fifteen model–ratio blocks, Mistral-Small-24B included
at every ratio; the two that carry fewer are itemised with their reasons in Section C.3. Against that

The per-task columns show what an average hides. A pruned model collects a floor of 0.375 on

The result does not depend on the field we compare against, the prompt format, or the preci-

AMP and 2SSP, leaves the ordering intact: CoCurve is first on seven of the eleven task columns and

with five demonstrations — the regime in which damage to attention shows first, and the reason
SlimGPT and 2SSP both report it — leaves it first on both models at both ratios (Table 24). Quantis-
ing the pruned model to 4 bits costs it about what the same step costs the dense model in accuracy,
so the two compressions draw on different slack rather than the same slack twice (Section D.9).
And the criterion’s own target is met: among the nine methods, ours reproduces the dense model’s
predictions most faithfully, first or second on all six tasks measured (Section D.7.1).

Compression is worth its accuracy cost only if it buys speed, so we measure it on physically removed
units rather than masks (Table 4): every timing runs on sliced weight matrices rather than masked
full-size ones (Section A.2). Removing 40% of the non-embedding parameters gives a 1.40× prefill
speedup at 0.66× peak memory on Llama-3.2-3B and 1.49× at 0.66× on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct,

fraction, structured methods land in one narrow band — Table 18 times all eight methods at ρ=20%
on one machine and their prefill speedups span 1.13 to 1.19×. What a method is worth is there-
fore the accuracy it delivers at that speedup (Table 1, Table 2). Selection cost does differ between
methods, and Section D.4.1 reports it.


3.5   W ITH A LIGHT RECOVERY STAGE

Past roughly 40%, no training-free method leaves a model within reach of dense accuracy. A prac-
titioner then asks what a short recovery stage buys, and whether a better selection still matters once
weights are allowed to move. One experiment answers both. We apply an identical LoRA stage, at
the same rank, token budget, schedule and corpus, to CoCurve and to four baselines at each of three
ratios. The corpus is disjoint from the calibration set. These models are no longer training-free, so
we report them separately from every other number in this paper (Table 5).
Recovery compresses the field, as it should — a random selection, once tuned, finishes above at least
two tuned baselines at every ratio, which is a caution against reading tuned numbers as evidence
about selection. What survives the compression is the ordering: CoCurve holds the best value in 15
of the 18 cells, and after tuning it has the highest seven-set commonsense average at all three ratios,
by 0.2, 1.8 and 1.5 points. A short recovery stage therefore does not substitute for choosing well; it
starts from wherever the selection left the model.

3.6   B EYOND ONE TOWER : VISION – LANGUAGE MODELS

Nothing in Section 2 is specific to a decoder-only stack. The risk is a KL divergence between
a frozen model and a masked copy of it; the estimator is a Gram matrix of single-unit ablation
features; the selector is a cost-normalised greedy under one budget. Point those three objects at a
vision–language model and the only thing that changes is that the unit inventory grows a second
tower. Everything else is held fixed: the same M single-unit ablations, the same Huv = D̄u⊤ D̄v /P ,
the same λ protocol of Section 2.4, the same guard, no gradients, no fine-tuning and no adapter.
One piece of bookkeeping is new — the layer index is composite, so the per-layer cap treats a
vision layer and a language layer as distinct layers rather than pooling them — and it introduces no
hyperparameter.

The edge term is not a regulariser that happens to help. It acts through a structure we can measure
and a population of units we can name, and deleting exactly that population reproduces the damage
on demand. Figure 3 is that argument end to end. The matrix has cross-module and long-range
mass on every model we estimate (a–d), and that is not a property of a decoder-only stack: the same
statistic on the 7 vision–language matrices of Section 3.6 puts the attention–FFN block at 0.93–0.99
of the within-module mean, against 0.74–0.98 on the text-only models, so panel (c) carries both
families. A two-tower model also measures the quantity a single stack cannot: coupling between
a language unit and a vision unit runs 0.58–0.89 of the coupling inside a tower, which is why one
shared budget across both towers is a measurement rather than a preference. That mass singles out
a minority of units node saliency cannot see (e). Forcing those units out costs nineteen of twenty
capability cells across five models (f). Nothing in the top row depends on a selection, a λ, or a
benchmark; it is a property of the estimated H.
The interactions node-first scoring discards are the same size as the ones it keeps. The curvature
correlation |ρuv | puts every entry of H on one scale, so the blocks are directly comparable. The
attention↔FFN block is not a correction term. It runs at 0.74 to 0.98 times the mean of the two
within-module blocks across five models (Figure 3c), and on Mistral-Nemo-12B it reaches 0.130
against 0.155 within attention. This is the quantitative content of treating pruning as a joint operation
across module types. It is also what makes a shared attention/FFN budget meaningful at all: without
a term coupling the two types, their saliencies live on incommensurable scales and any joint ranking
is arbitrary.

Deleting exactly those units reproduces the damage. Structure in H is not yet evidence about the
model, so we force the issue, on every model we build H for. We prune the bridge units outright
against a control matched unit-for-unit on saliency, unit type and parameter cost but drawn from the
low-coupling population. One greedy fill, computed once and shared by both arms, completes them,

and WikiText perplexity improves by 4.5.
That dissociation is the mechanism in its sharpest form, and it is not one model’s accident. Across
five models, nineteen of the twenty capability cells fall, by up to 23.7 points, and the largest model
gives the largest effect: on Mistral-Small-24B all four groups fall by 14.8 to 23.7 (Figure 3f, per-
model numbers in Table 23). On three of the five, perplexity moves the other way while every
capability group in that row falls, so the arm a next-token proxy prefers is the arm that has lost the
most capability. The claim is not that perplexity always points the wrong way — on Mistral-Small-
24B the bridge removal costs 425 perplexity as well, and there the two agree — but that nothing
makes it point the right way, and on the majority of models here it does not. What node-first scoring
mis-reads is not how important a unit is on its own but what it is holding together, and the proxy
such scoring is built on cannot be relied on to notice. This is also why Section 3.2 reports the two
families side by side rather than leading with the one our objective is closest to: the same caution
applies to our own numbers, and the capability columns are the ones we ask to be believed.
Given one budget, the objective writes its own allocation, and writes the same one every time.

What this asks of the field. Structured pruning has spent its effort on better node scores, and a term
none of them contain sets the ceiling on that programme. The pair term is measurable in any dense
Transformer, it costs one ablation per unit and no gradients, and the units it protects can be located
in a model before anything is pruned. The question to ask is not which units matter, but which units
matter together. The object of study that follows is the matrix, not the ranking.


5    R ELATED W ORK

Training-free structured pruning of LLMs. The dominant paradigm scores each removable unit
independently and deletes the lowest-ranked ones, using first/second-order Taylor importance (Ma
et al., 2023), activation statistics (An et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024), rotate-and-slice projec-
tions (Ashkboos et al., 2024), per-layer OBS (Ling et al., 2024; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023; 2022),
or least-squares output reconstruction (Guo et al., 2025). A parallel line prunes whole layers (Men
et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024) or searches a global layer-wise budget (Li et al.,
2025; Jansen et al., 2026). All share a node-first assumption. Set-level pruning damage is treated as
additive, and even when several module types are scored they are merged into one independent rank-
ing. These are our baselines and concurrent comparisons. CoCurve departs by making the cross-unit
interaction a first-class, derived quantity. Table 9 positions it against interaction-aware and modern
structured pruners.
Beyond the diagonal: interaction-aware pruning. CoCurve descends from classical second-order
saliency (LeCun et al., 1989; Hassibi & Stork, 1993) and Fisher/Taylor importance for modern
networks (Theis et al., 2018; Molchanov et al., 2019). Diagonal CoCurve is an OBD-style structured
saliency (Eq. 4). Curvature beyond the diagonal is not new either. What CoCurve contributes is a


                                                           17
Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




specific object, and it is worth naming its properties because they are what the rest of this section
is measured against: an attention↔FFN, cross-layer Fisher block at unit-pair granularity, recovered
from M single-unit ablations with no gradients and no pairwise sweep, and used to decide which
individual units go under a training-free selection-only solve.
Each of the four properties is where a near neighbour stops. LLM-Surgeon’s KFAC curvature
is Kronecker-factored within a single weight matrix and explicitly disregards cross-layer interac-
tions (van der Ouderaa et al., 2024): no cross-module block. SlimGPT models coupling inside a
layer and compensates as it prunes (Ling et al., 2024), and ZipLM couples layers only through a
runtime-aware sparsity profile rather than an explicit curvature term (Kurtic et al., 2023): no cross-
layer curvature, and neither is selection-only. SAViT comes closest in spirit — an explicit cross-
component second-order block for vision Transformers — but collapses each block to its scalar
mean (Zheng et al., 2022), so its interaction sets how much of each component type to remove while
the choice among individual units stays independent: no unit-pair granularity. Two works come at
the object we build from opposite sides, and together they say why it has stayed out of reach. Kwon
et al. (2022) write the pairwise Fisher between unit mask variables and note in as many words that
the interaction between two heads that cover for one another lives in its non-diagonal elements —
then approximate that matrix block-diagonally, one block per attention or FFN layer, with no in-
teraction between mask variables in different layers. Yang et al. (2023) write the same object for
vision Transformers, observe that a model has tens of thousands of structural groups, declare com-
puting it directly infeasible, and retreat to a per-unit norm. The block CoCurve estimates is exactly
what both of them excluded, and the Gram construction of Section 2.2 is why we can afford it: M
forward ablations, no gradients, no pairwise sweep. Section E.1 does the same accounting for the
remaining neighbours — SOSP (Nonnenmacher et al., 2022), the Combinatorial Brain Surgeon (Yu
et al., 2022), whose weight-level cancellation argument is the ancestor of ours, and Optimal Brain
Connection (Chen et al., 2025), which assumes the block-diagonal form we discard.
Recovering what compression costs. Restoring capability after pruning belongs to pipelines that
retrain, such as the distillation and continued pretraining of Minitron (Muralidharan et al., 2024)
or evolutionary search (Tang et al., 2025). That is a different regime from the selection-only one
studied here, where the question is which units to remove before any weight moves (Jaiswal et al.,
2024). Section 3.5 reports what a light recovery stage adds when it is enabled for every method at
once.
Compressing a vision–language model. The dominant axis there is the visual token sequence
rather than the weights: FastV (Chen et al., 2024a), SparseVLM (Zhang et al., 2025) and
MADTP (Cao et al., 2024) shorten the sequence a decoder attends to and leave every parameter
in place. That is orthogonal to removing structure — one shortens the input, the other shrinks the
network — and the two compose. Where vision–language work does remove weights it trains:
UPop (Shi et al., 2023) searches subnets and retrains progressively, and EfficientLLaVA (Liang
et al., 2025) searches a per-component pruning policy while optimising the vision projector. Sec-
tion 3.6 is the training-free structural setting in that picture, and the property that makes it a test of
this paper rather than a second paper is that one H spans both towers, so the budget split between
them is an output of the objective.


6    C ONCLUSION

Structured LLM pruning has been operating at the wrong granularity: it ranks units as independent
nodes, while Transformer computation couples them through a shared residual stream. CoCurve
replaces that ranking with global co-pruning-risk minimisation, from a single Fisher matrix that is
node saliency and cross-module co-pruning curvature at once, recovered from single-unit ablations
and solved in one shot under a shared budget. How much to trust the off-diagonal is read off a
finite path of solutions by a held-out, label-free criterion, using the same rule at every compression
ratio. Across five models from 3B to 24B and three compression ratios, CoCurve leads on 38 of 45
perplexity cells and 32 of 60 capability-group cells against the per-column best of every baseline,
and on 666 of 721 cells against those baselines one at a time. The margin is largest where the field
breaks: on Mistral-Small-24B at ρ=40% its perplexity is 277.1 against the best baseline’s 3299. Our
ablations attribute the gain to the off-diagonal rather than to node saliency, and it survives a control
that fixes the attention–FFN split at the diagonal selector’s own allocation. The term acts through a

population we can name and delete on demand, and it carries positional structure the diagonal does
not: coupling falls with depth on all ten models, while saliency does not even keep its sign. Under
one shared budget the objective then recovers, without being told to, an allocation that spends the
first half of the depth on FFN width alone: of the twenty-five solutions across five models and five
ratios, seventeen remove attention at all, and thirteen of those take none of it from the first half.
None of this is tied to one modality. The same three objects — a KL risk, a Gram matrix of single-
unit ablations, a budgeted greedy — transfer to vision–language models unchanged, where they
also measure something a single stack cannot: the curvature coupling a language unit to a vision
unit, which runs 0.58–0.89 of the coupling inside a tower and is why one budget shared across both
towers is a measurement rather than a preference.
Limitations and outlook. Estimating the edge matrix costs hours rather than minutes, but only once
per model. It is independent of the ratio and of λ, so one estimate serves the whole path. Turning
a mask into a smaller model needs a slicing path per operator family. What a removable unit is is
the open question: under mixture-of-experts routing the units are already sparsely activated, and in
stacks that interleave attention with linear-recurrent layers some layers have no heads to remove.
The risk of Eq. 1 is defined in both cases; the inventory it is minimised over is not, and extending
it there is the natural next step. The inventory changes; the object does not. Wherever units share a
residual stream, the damage from removing a set is not the sum of the damages, and the term that
says so is measurable without a single gradient: prune the edges, not just the nodes.

FFN groups. The intermediate dimension is partitioned into nf contiguous groups. Because the
map from the (gated) intermediate activation to the FFN output is linear, the output is exactly the
sum of per-group contributions, so pruning group j removes the rows of Wgate , Wup (or W1 ) and
the columns of Wdown (or W2 ) for that group. The runtime mask zeros the gated product before the
down projection; equivalently it zeros those up/gate rows, since the SwiGLU/GELU gate satisfies
σ(0) · (·) = 0.
Mask boundary and equivalence. A runtime mask zeros only a unit’s residual-stream contribution
and never alters normalisation, RoPE, residual addition, causal/padding masks, GQA repeat rules,
or dropout (the model is in eval mode). Two things follow, and we check them separately. Zeroing
a unit’s weight slices is bit-identical to masking it at runtime, which we verify on a held-out batch,
so a masked evaluation and a zeroed one are the same measurement. Slicing the weight matrices
instead is what the timings run on, and its correctness is checked by the realised parameter count of
every timed model: on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at ρ=20% the sliced model holds 6.633B parameters
against the dense 8.030B, and the logit equivalence of the slicing path is verified on a synthetic
grouped-query Llama to 10−4 . Reported speedups therefore come from a smaller model, not from
zeroed weights in a full-size one.

Algorithm 1 details the estimator. The crux (Proposition 3) is that gathering every unit’s ablated

Top-r coordinate consistency. Using a per-unit top-r would place different units in different coor-
dinate systems and invalidate the Gram; the shared teacher support is essential. Defaults: r=256,
128 C4 sequences of length 2048, deterministic token subsampling (Ptok =128, so P = 16384
stored positions). Increasing r beyond 256 leaves H nearly unchanged (Section D.5).
Optional prefix-cache replay. An exact speedup caches residual states before the masked compu-

ablations from the pre-FFN residual. Multiple masks can share an input batch via the batch dimen-

Remark (cost-normalised selection). Choosing u by ∆R(u | S)/cu is the density-ordering heuristic
for the underlying min-quadratic knapsack, the standard surrogate when an exact solve is intractable,
and the shared budget lets the attention-vs-FFN split emerge from the objective rather than a hand-

every run we inspect: Huu ≥ 0 exactly (Proposition 3) and all 11,325 in-set edges are positive on

ordered by ∆ rather than by ∆/cu , since dividing a negative gain by cost prefers the cheaper unit.

C.2   M ODELS AND E XPERIMENTAL P ROTOCOL

All models use GQA (attention units are KV-aligned query-head groups); FFN groups are nf =16
per layer; cost is removable parameter count. The unit definition is a property of the comparison
rather than of any method: every selector here, ours included, chooses among the same units under
the same budget.
Keeping the removal distributed. We prevent a selector from concentrating its budget in a few
layers, using a per-layer cap κ on removed cost together with protection of the first two and last two
layers. This is not a device we introduced: it is a weaker version of what the strongest baseline in
our set already does in its published form. LLM-Pruner reports that the first and last layers dominate
performance, and its released configuration accordingly leaves the first three layers and the final
layer untouched and applies a uniform ratio to every layer in between — for a 20% target, 25% from
layer 5 to layer 30 of 32 (Ma et al., 2023). Our guard protects four layers instead of four-and-a-half
and then lets the objective allocate freely up to κ inside the rest, rather than fixing one ratio per
layer. It is the looser constraint, and it is applied to every method equally. It applies to every method
compared and to every ratio, by one rule used throughout,
                           κ = 1.5 ρ,       first and last two layers protected.
The rule has two properties. First it has no free parameter and no reference ratio, so nothing is
tuned per model or extrapolated from one setting to another. Second, and more important for the
comparison, it guarantees the target is attainable: with |Lp | protected layers of L the reachable ratio
is at most (1 − |Lp |/L) κ, which for |Lp |=4 and κ=1.5ρ exceeds ρ whenever L > 12, true of
every model here. A guard chosen without that check silently prevents a method from reaching the
requested ratio: a per-layer cap that ignores the protected layers leaves a shortfall of up to 6 points
at ρ=0.5, which flatters the affected method, since it is then compared while less compressed. We


                                                    35
Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




therefore verify the realised ratio of every mask before evaluating it and report only masks within
0.2 points of their target. That tolerance is not a round number: Proposition 7 bounds the overshoot
of the stopping rule by the largest single unit’s share of the cost, which is 0.13 to 0.20 points on the
models here, so a mask that misses its target by more than that has missed it for some reason other
than unit granularity.
Switching the guard off. The rule is one line, but it is still a choice, so we removed it and measured:
no cap, no protected layers, and λ̂ re-derived in the unguarded family, on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at
ρ=20%. Three things follow. It does not interact with the calibration: λ̂ moves from 0.0108 to
0.0098. It is a protection rather than a handicap: unguarded, FLAP and Wanda-sp delete the first
seven layers outright and collapse, to WikiText 41,189 and 30,742 with every capability group at
or below chance, so the cap and the first-layer protection are what let them be compared at all.
And it is not shaped to suit us: our own solution concentrates too without it — eight layers past
30% of their removable cost and one at 76% — and loses capability, while LLM-Pruner, whose
unguarded solution instead takes nothing before layer 18, gains on multiple choice, the direction
reported for depth pruning (Men et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024). What the guard changes is therefore
how concentrated a solution may be, and the selectors do not agree on whether concentration helps;
what it does not do is give any of them a protocol the comparison could have used instead. The layer
protection in particular is not load-bearing for CoCurve: with the guard off the objective still takes
nothing from the first two layers, and 5% and 2% from the last two.

C.3   BASELINE M ETHODS

All baselines are training-free, run under identical budgets and harness with no recovery.
Every baseline is given our allocator, not only our budget. A baseline here is its own importance
metric driving our global cost-normalised greedy with our per-layer cap, free to spend the budget
across layers and across module types exactly as CoCurve does. Several of these methods do not
allocate globally in their published form — FLAP and Wanda-sp threshold per layer, LLM-Pruner
works over coupled groups — so the harness gives them allocation freedom they do not otherwise
have, and any remaining difference is the importance metric alone. Two arms keep their own al-
location because ours would misrepresent them: SlimLLM, whose head and FFN scores are not
comparable across module types, and 2SSP, whose two stages are the method. Nothing here is tuned
per model.
And a finer unit definition, where that is what a baseline wants. The shared nf =16 is a property
of the comparison, but three baselines — Wanda-sp, FLAP and Magnitude — score channels na-
tively, so the shared definition could be costing them resolution while suiting us: our own selection
costs M single-unit ablations, so we need M in the thousands and they do not. We therefore re-ran
those three at nf =64, four times finer than the shared definition and four times finer than our own
method could afford, at the same ratio, guard and harness. Resolution was indeed part of what held
two of them back: Wanda-sp improves from 20.02 to 14.86 WikiText perplexity and on all four
capability groups, and FLAP from 20.88 to 16.23 and on three of the four, while Magnitude gets
worse on four of the five measures. Even so, at four times our resolution the best of the three is still
2.0 perplexity and 0.6 to 3.8 points per capability group behind our nf =16 selection, so resolution

Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




is not what the gap is made of. Random prunes structured units uniformly (lower bound). Mag-
nitude (Han et al., 2015) scores units by weight norm. Wanda-sp, the structured generalisation of
Wanda (Sun et al., 2024) introduced and named by An et al. (2024), is the structured input-norm–
weighted magnitude. FLAP (An et al., 2024) scores units by activation-fluctuation importance,
Var(a) · ∥W ∥2 per column. LLM-Pruner (Ma et al., 2023) applies a Taylor (first-order with diago-
nal Fisher) group importance over coupled weight slices; its gradient pass reads the same calibration
text at 512 tokens per sequence, the short-sequence regime the method is defined in. SlimGPT (Ling
et al., 2024) and Which blocks carry fewer than seven baselines. Two of the fifteen model–ratio
blocks of Table 2 compare against fewer than the full set, and in both cases the reason is a property
of the baseline rather than a choice of ours. On Mistral-Nemo-12B at ρ=20% LLM-Pruner exceeds
the memory of the card it was run on (Section D.4.1). On Falcon3-7B at ρ=30% SlimLLM keeps its
own per-layer, per-type allocation, which cannot spend an attention budget smaller than one of that
model’s four units per layer, so its mask lands 0.8 points off target and the ratio band of Section C.2
excludes it. Every other block compares against all seven, Mistral-Small-24B at all three reported
ratios included. In the wider sweep of Table 17 that model carries one baseline at ρ=10% and five
at ρ=50%, ratios outside the three the main comparison reports.
SlimLLM (Guo et al., 2025) scores heads by the Pearson similarity of the sublayer output with
and without each head and FFN channels in a PCA eigenbasis, then restores performance by least-
squares regression on each sublayer’s output matrix — a reconstruction step, but not an OBS-style
weight surgery. It keeps its own per-layer ratio allocation and per-module ranking rather than being
forced into a global one, since its head and FFN scores are not comparable across module types; that
allocation is per layer and per unit type, so on Falcon3-7B, which has four attention units per layer, it
cannot spend an attention budget of less than one unit and two of its runs fall outside the ratio band
and are excluded from the comparison. AMP (Mugnaini et al., 2025) prunes a uniform fraction
of attention heads and MLP neurons in every layer, scoring units by the ℓ1 norm of calibration
activations projected through the output weights. 2SSP (Sandri et al., 2025) is a two-stage structured
pruner that removes FFN width in a first stage and whole attention submodules by depth in a second;
it argues that pruning within attention at neuron or width granularity is impractical under the scaled
dot-product. We additionally report two single-module variants of our pipeline (head-only, FFN-
only) and the diagonal-only (λ=0) variant. For methods originally including LoRA recovery, it
is disabled for the no-recovery comparison. The two most recent methods, AMP and 2SSP, are
compared on the headline Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, where their released configurations apply directly;
Table 15 carries both, AMP inside the shared guard and 2SSP with its own two-stage budget. 2SSP is
the strongest of the nine on ARC-easy and ahead of CoCurve on PTB perplexity, where LLM-Pruner
is ahead of both. The cross-model study (Table 2, Section D.1) uses the seven established baselines,
which port across every architecture studied here under the shared harness without method-specific
re-tuning. On Mistral-Small-24B the comparison carries all seven at ρ=30%, at ρ=20% and at
ρ=40%; SlimGPT’s and LLM-Pruner’s numbers there come from the streamed re-implementation
of Section D.4.1, which is numerically identical to the published form.

D.3       T HE R ECOVERY S TAGE

Selection-only is the setting this paper studies, and every number outside this subsection has weight
repair disabled for every method. Table 5 reports what happens when it is enabled: one LoRA stage,

Recovery compresses the field. Random, at or near the bottom before tuning at every ratio, finishes

from the recovery rather than from which units were removed, and the ordering survives it. After

is the question a practitioner asks before paying for the selection at all.

D.4     E FFICIENCY

Table 4 reports parameter reduction, prefill and decode throughput and peak memory from real
physical removal (Section A.2). Table 18 resolves the same measurement method by method on the
headline model, and the result is the one the body states: at iso-budget the gain is governed by the re-
moved non-embedding parameter fraction, so it is shared across selectors rather than distinguishing
them: prefill spans only 1.13–1.19× and decode 1.06–1.17× across the eight methods, with peak
memory identical to within 0.07 GB. Prefill behaves this way on every model we time (1.17–1.21×
for CoCurve over the four architectures timed here) because it is compute-bound and the removed
non-embedding fraction is matched. Single-stream decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and is not
a band: on the smallest model, Llama-3.2-3B, five of the eight selectors decode slower than dense
(down to 0.70×) because their per-layer widths leave badly shaped kernels, while CoCurve keeps
1.10× and is second of the nine rows. So throughput does not separate selectors where the hardware
is compute-bound, and where it does separate them it favours us; either way the discriminator is
accuracy at a given speedup (Figure 2). The pruning wall-clock is a one-time cost dominated by the
single-unit ablation pass, which carries no gradients or labels and shards trivially across devices; it
is paid once per model.
Two of Table 4’s cells are deliberately blank, and the two ρ=40% rows above them are what stands
in for them: both were timed here, on an otherwise idle fleet, with the same physical removal and
the same mask the accuracy tables report. The Mistral-Small-24B row’s throughput was timed on
the second machine before that model’s mask was corrected, and prefill and decode depend on the
attention/FFN split, which the correction moved on 64% of the removed set at that ratio; reprinting
a throughput number measured against a mask this paper no longer reports would be worse than
leaving it out. Its parameter and peak-memory columns are printed: at this scale the peak is weights,
and the removed non-embedding fraction is set by the budget rather than by which units the solver
chose. A throughput measurement is only as good as the quiet of the machine it runs on — the
same fleet, timed while three neighbouring cards were busy, reported a dense reference 40% below
its idle value and prefill speedups scattered from 1.20 to 1.70× where an idle run gives the tight
1.13–1.19× band above — so the numbers in this table were taken with the fleet otherwise idle,
and any run whose method-to-method spread exceeds 0.25× is discarded as contended rather than
published.

comparison this is the cheap end. LLM-Surgeon reports 17h08m on four H100s for a 7B model,
68.5 GPU-hours, and 267 GPU-hours at 13B (van der Ouderaa et al., 2024); we spend 12.6 GPU-
hours on a single card at 8B.
One build serves every operating point. H depends on the model, the calibration set and the unit
definition, not on ρ and not on λ, so one build serves the whole ratio sweep and the whole regular-
isation path. This distinguishes us from reconstruction-based selectors, not from activation-based
ones: a per-unit norm is also ratio-independent and can be thresholded anywhere, so the amortisation
argument is about SlimGPT, SlimLLM and SliceGPT rather than about Wanda-sp or FLAP. Against
the former, SlimGPT’s own timings show the dependence: 678 s at 20% against 1074 s at 50% on a
7B model (Ling et al., 2024). The comparable unit is therefore cost per delivered operating point,
and over the five ratios we report our build is 151 minutes per point against SlimGPT’s 82.
And one configuration serves the whole family. The guard has no free parameter (κ=1.5ρ, two
protected layers at each end) and λ is selected by Eq. 11 rather than tuned, so the same configuration
produces every model at every ratio in this paper: five models, five ratios, one setting. None of
the timings above — ours or anyone else’s — include the cost of finding a per-model or per-ratio
configuration, and that omission is the one that favours us.
It is forward-only, and at 24B that is the difference between running and not. No backward pass,
no optimiser state, no labels: peak memory during selection is inference memory, so a model that
fits for evaluation fits for pruning. The two strongest baselines do not have this property. Neither of
the two strongest baselines is expensive at the scales their papers report, and both become infeasible
at 24B for a reason that is structural rather than incidental. LLM-Pruner backpropagates through the
whole model, so it holds the weights and a full set of gradients at once: 2 × 47.1 = 94.3 GB against
the 79.3 GiB an A100-80GB makes available. SlimGPT is layer-wise and needs only about 7 GB of
device memory at 7B, but its host-side accumulator is a d × d float64 covariance per layer, and at
d=5120 over 40 layers that is 8.59 × 40 = 344 GB against a 128 GB host. Run in their published
form at 24B, both abort, at measured peaks of 83.9 GB device memory and 125 GB resident host
memory.
Their 24B numbers in this paper exist because we re-implemented both to stream. The group count
is a run parameter set from the host budget: SlimGPT’s covariance is collected in eight groups of
five layers, which brings its peak from 344 to 43 GB, and LLM-Pruner’s backward is split into three,
keeping a third of the gradients resident at a time. Neither change touches what is computed, since
a per-layer covariance and a per-weight gradient do not depend on how the layers are grouped, so
the scores are identical to the published form. What changes is that one pass becomes several. That
multi-pass overhead is the price of estimating curvature from gradients at this scale, and it is exactly
what a forward-only estimator does not pay.

The edge estimator has two finite-budget knobs: the calibration size (number of sampled positions)

Bridge units. With du = Huu and bu = v̸=u |Huv |, bridge units (low du , high bu ) are retained

A concrete bridge unit. To make the phenomenon tangible, we single out one unit from the real
estimated H of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. A representative bridge is an FFN channel group at layer 1.
Its node saliency Huu places it in the bottom 31% of all units, so a node-first scorer∑︁   (magnitude,
activation, or diagonal Taylor) would rank it as safely prunable. Yet its coupling bu = v̸=u |Huv |
is in the top decile: its strongest co-pruning edges run to attention units at layer 0 (a cross-module
coupling, |Huv | ≈ 5.2 × 10−3 , comparable to its own saliency 7.4 × 10−3 ) and to FFN groups at
layers 1–3 (cross-layer). This is exactly the configuration CoCurve is built to catch: a unit whose
removal is cheap in isolation but costly alongside the early-attention and nearby-FFN units it is
coupled to. Diagonal pruning deletes it together with those partners; CoCurve retains it.
Controlled causal test: bridge connectivity, not saliency, drives the damage. The bridge diag-
nosis above is structural, read off H; we confirm it is causal with a matched-pair intervention, run
on every model we build H for. On each, we force-prune the bridge units (low du , high bu ) and,
as a control, an equal-size set drawn from the same low-saliency pool but with low connectivity,
matched unit-for-unit on saliency du , unit type and per-unit cost. The remainder of the budget is
one cost-normalised greedy fill, computed once and shared by both arms. The two masks that result
hold the same number of units, the same attention/FFN split, the same removed cost and the same
realised ratio to the last bit, and differ only in which low-saliency population was forced out, so any
gap isolates connectivity with saliency, type, cost and ratio all held fixed. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct

The selection criterion is token-level KL to the dense model, so the first thing to check is whether the
pruned model actually reproduces the dense model’s decisions more faithfully than the alternatives

Splitting those items by whether dense was right sharpens it: on PIQA, CoCurve reproduces the

selection delivers the fidelity its objective asks for, and it does so without the benchmark ever being
consulted.

D.9     C OMPOSITION WITH Q UANTISATION

Pruning and quantisation attack different redundancies: one removes units, the other lowers the
precision of what remains. Whether they compose is a deployment question, and it is not settled by
either measurement alone. If the two drew on the same slack, stacking them would cost more than
their separate costs suggest.
Table 25 pairs each model state with itself at bf16 and at 4 bits, so the cost of quantisation is a within-
row difference and the dense row is the reference for what that cost should be. On the dense model 4
bits costs 5.7% of perplexity and 0.8 multiple-choice points. After pruning to ρ=30% it costs 7.0%
and 1.8 points, and at ρ=40% 13.1% and 1.3 points. The accuracy cost is therefore roughly what
the dense model pays at both ratios, while the perplexity cost grows as the remaining slack is spent,
which is what a redundancy account predicts, and small enough that the two compressions can be
applied together.
The comparison arms turn this from a property of the pipeline into a property of the selection. The
two strongest baselines are quantised from their own pruned models under the identical quantiser
and load order, and the ordering after 4 bits is the ordering before: CoCurve reaches 23.1 perplexity
and 49.9 accuracy, against 29.2 and 48.8 for LLM-Pruner and 80.0 and 44.9 for SlimLLM.
Quantisation is close to additive with the selection, so a later decision to quantise does not make the
choice of which units to remove for the practitioner.

D.10    ROBUSTNESS OF THE S ELECTION

Three questions about stability, each answered by a different perturbation: resampling the held-out
set, changing the selection protocol, and rebuilding the curvature from independent calibration text.
The third costs a full re-estimation, which we paid.

D.11    H OW STABLE IS THE SELECTED λ?

Every number in this paper comes from one calibration draw and one held-out set of 16 sequences.
Two questions follow, and they are different: whether the selection step would survive a different
draw of the held-out set, and whether the curvature it selects over would survive a different draw of
the calibration text. We answer both.

Resampling the held-out set collapses the candidate space. The path files record the per-
sequence risk at every path point, so the bootstrap is exact and needs no further computation. Over
26 paths spanning ten models and five ratios, at 2000 resamples each, the resampled criterion reaches
a median of 3.3% of the members of the enumerated family, and 2.5% at the three ratios this paper
reports. A typical family holds 318 prunings and the whole bootstrap moves among ten of them.
The 5–95 percentile of λ̂ spans a median of 0.016 of [0, 1], and is under 0.05 on 16 of the 26 paths.
What the criterion delivers is a pruning at the minimum rather than a distinguished value of λ:
evaluated back on the full held-out set, the mask a resample chooses costs a median of 0.00%
additional risk over the one λ̂ chooses, and every path puts at least 98.5% of resamples within one
standard error of the minimum. The solution is piecewise constant and the path is flat near its
optimum, so the members the bootstrap moves among are the ones that cannot be told apart.

Does the selected point hold up downstream? The bootstrap judges λ̂ by held-out risk, the quan-
tity it is selected on. The independent check is to take the selected point and its neighbours and
evaluate all of them on the full suite. On Llama-3.2-3B at ρ=20% we do this for five points: λ̂ itself,
the two members at the edges of its 1-SE band, and the two nearest solutions just outside it. The
selected point is within 0.3 points of the best of the five on three of the four capability groups and
within 0.91 on the fourth, and it is 2.7 to 2.9 points above the worst on the twelve-task mean. The
criterion lands at the favourable end of the region it cannot resolve.

Changing the protocol. A bootstrap only perturbs the sample. We also varied the protocol itself
on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at ρ=20%, one change at a time: the selection statistic (KL → NLL),
the number of held-out sequences (16 → 48, pooling three independent draws), and the draw itself
(two further seeds). Four of these five variants select the bit-identical solution, λ̂ = 0.0108. The
exception is changing the held-out domain from C4 to WikiText, which moves λ̂ to 0.0213: a
different breakpoint, the same order of magnitude, and the direction one would predict when the
evaluation corpus changes.

Holding the curvature fixed. Both perturbations above act on the selection while H stays as

D.12    R EBUILDING THE CURVATURE FROM INDEPENDENT CALIBRATION TEXT

Section D.11 perturbs the held-out set and the selection protocol but holds H fixed. The question
it cannot reach is whether a curvature estimate built from different text would lead anywhere else.
Answering it means paying the full estimation cost a second time, which we did. On Llama-3.2-3B
we rebuilt H from scratch (activation collection, all 672 single-unit ablations, Gram assembly) from
a disjoint draw of the same sources, with its own held-out file, and re-ran the identical protocol with
no parameter changed.
What the selector consumes transfers. Diagonal saliency du correlates at 0.99 between the two
draws, and total coupling bu , the quantity the bridge-unit argument rests on, at 0.96. This is the level
at which the method reads H: the marginal of Eq. 8 contracts the matrix against the selected set, and
Section B.3.2 shows the same contraction is what the split-half reproduction measures. Individual
entries transfer less well than their aggregates, and the selector consumes the aggregates.
The selection moves, and the model does not. λ̂ goes from 0.0911 to 0.0383 and the two prunings
share 57.9% of their removed units. At ρ=20% no capability group moves by more than 0.81 points
(commonsense −0.30, science QA +0.16, reading +0.42, world knowledge +0.81) and the twelve-
task mean moves +0.15. Perplexity is within 2.1% on two of three corpora (−0.3% WikiText,
−2.1% C4) and +14.0% on PTB, the least stable of the three here as elsewhere.
The three levels together say more than agreement would have. What the selector reads off H
transfers (du at 0.99, bu at 0.96); the tie-breaking among units the marginal cannot separate does
not, and does not need to. The held-out bootstrap shows the same structure from the other direction:
mask agreement of 58.7% alongside a median excess risk of 0.00%. What transfers is the aggregate,
which is what the method is asked to deliver.

Units and masking. Units are attention heads — grouped by shared key/value head where the tower
uses grouped-query attention — and FFN channel groups, sixteen groups per layer, in both towers. A
unit is removed by zeroing its slice of the input to the projection that writes into the residual stream,
exactly as in the text-only pipeline. The layer index is composite, so the per-layer cap κ=1.5ρ and
the first/last-two-layer protection apply within each tower rather than to a pooled index. Nothing
else is added: no per-tower budget, no tower-level weighting, and no new hyperparameter.
Calibration. 128 image–caption pairs from Flickr30k (Young et al., 2014) — 192 for Qwen2-VL-
2B and 96 for LLaVA-NeXT-7B, whose matrices were estimated under earlier budgets and left as
they were. The difference is a build-time budget on our own estimator and nothing else: every
baseline draws its own fixed calibration on every model alike (64 pairs for Wanda-sp and FLAP,
32 for LLM-Pruner’s gradient pass), and the 48 held-out pairs used to choose λ are taken from the
same deterministic stream immediately after that model’s own calibration draw — pairs 128–175
where the matrix saw 128 or fewer, pairs 192–239 on Qwen2-VL-2B — so on every model they
are images the estimator never saw. Neither departure moves a comparison: a draw below 128
makes the off-diagonal noisier and the held-out criterion correspondingly more reluctant to trust
it, which costs our arm and no other; and the one model built on more carries the median of our
five headline margins, not the largest. The held-out pairs are used only for the λ argmin and enter
nothing else. Flickr rather than COCO is deliberate: SEED-Bench and several neighbouring suites
are built on COCO images, so a COCO calibration draw could place an evaluation image in the
estimator. Scored positions are the caption tokens only — the prompt and the image placeholders
are context, and scoring them would let a unit look important for reproducing text the model was
handed. The teacher support is top-r=64 rather than the 256 used in the text-only experiments,
because an image-grounded sequence is expensive; splitting the scored positions into disjoint halves
gives two independent estimates of H that correlate at r=0.99 on the off-diagonal, so the reduction
costs nothing that matters here.

Recovery is not a fixed increment added to whatever selection produced. It is a second optimisation
whose reachable endpoint depends on what the mask left behind, and at a budget where no arm
retains a quarter of its dense score above chance before training, that dependence is what decides
the ordering — not the selection-only scores, which are indistinguishable there.
Two things follow. Where a recovery budget is spent, the ranking it produces is its own: it agrees
with the selection-only ranking on the model that still holds most of its dense score and departs from
it on the one that does not. Selection and recovery are therefore optimising different things at the
highest budget, and mixing them would report neither cleanly — which is why the body measures
selection alone, the quantity this paper’s estimator predicts, and why this table is reported separately
rather than folded into Table 6. The second reading is the practical one: with recovery, ρ=40%
becomes usable at all. On Qwen3-VL-8B every recovered arm but Magnitude ends above 46.99, the
best selection-only score any baseline reaches at ρ=30%, and the strongest recovered 40% model
— LLM-Pruner’s, at 59.8 — edges past the strongest unrecovered 30% one, ours at 57.7.

Section 5 names the three closest structured methods; the remaining near neighbours differ from
CoCurve on a defining property of Table 9. SOSP (Nonnenmacher et al., 2022) is the closest prior
work on the interaction axis and deserves a precise statement. Its SOSP-I variant does build a full
pairwise structure–structure matrix and selects individual structures with it; its scalable SOSP-H
variant reduces this to one scalar per structure and, in the authors’ words, cannot account for individ-
ual pairwise correlations. Both require a backward pass — Jacobians for SOSP-I, a Hessian-vector
product for SOSP-H — both linearise a supervised training loss rather than a label-free divergence to
the dense model, both take structures of one kind (filters, neurons, whole layers) rather than hetero-
geneous attention and FFN units, and both fine-tune after pruning. Its Appendix C also derives, for
interpretation, an identity of the same shape as our Eq. 5: a per-structure output feature contracted
through the softmax Fisher. That identity is obtained from the positive homogeneity of ReLU net-
works and is stated for networks without normalisation layers, so it does not hold for a Transformer,
and it is not the route SOSP computes — the algorithm evaluates ∇θ fθ . Our construction instead


                                                   55
Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




measures δzu by finite-difference ablation, which assumes nothing about the activation and needs no
gradient; that is what makes the same shape available at 24B. The Combinatorial Brain Surgeon (Yu
et al., 2022) is the honest ancestor of the argument in Section 1: it observes that the damage from
pruning one weight depends on which others go, and asks for removals whose effects cancel. It does
so between individual weights, from backpropagated gradients, and for large models falls back to the
same layer-wise diagonal chunks as WoodFisher (Singh & Alistarh, 2020), so no cross-module or
cross-layer term survives; its full form also updates the retained weights. What CoCurve contributes
is not the cancellation argument but a form of it that is computable at structured-unit granularity
across module types, from forward passes alone, with nothing retrained. Two data points from that
literature explain why the granularity matters: Frantar et al. (2021) report that for unstructured prun-
ing the effect of correlation is small and the gains come mostly from the weight update, and Yu et al.
(2022) need a mixed-integer solve to exploit it at all. At structured-unit granularity, with weight
updates forbidden, the interaction is the only lever left — and Section 3.3 is what it is worth. Opti-
mal Brain Connection (Chen et al., 2025), despite the name, assumes J ⊤ J is block diagonal so that
only parameters inside one filter or channel interact — the cross-unit terms we keep are the ones
it drops — and it needs Jacobians, fine-tuning and extra autoencoder parameters. It should not be
confused with Optimal Brain Compression (Frantar & Alistarh, 2022). On the attention/FFN axis,
AMP (Mugnaini et al., 2025) co-prunes both module types yet scores each unit independently and
LoRA-recovers, while 2SSP (Sandri et al., 2025) prunes FFN width then attention in two disjoint
stages. The concurrent D2 Prune (Xiong et al., 2026) shares the “dual/Taylor” name but discards the
off-diagonal cross terms and is unstructured.
Beyond Section 5, we note three further connections. (i) Unstructured/semi-structured pruning
(SparseGPT (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023), Wanda (Sun et al., 2024), OBC (Frantar & Alistarh, 2022))
optimises per-layer reconstruction at the weight level; CoCurve instead optimises a global, output-
distribution objective over structured units, and is hardware-friendly, requiring no sparse kernels.
(ii) Depth/width search methods (DarwinLM (Tang et al., 2025), CFSP (Wang et al., 2024b)) ex-
plore architectures by repeated evaluation; CoCurve is single-shot with a closed-form surrogate.
Bonsai (Kolawole et al., 2024) belongs with neither group and is the closest work to ours on the
estimation axis: it too scores structured units from forward passes only, with no gradients and no
weight update, and it too reasons globally across layers rather than layer by layer. What it fits to
those passes is an additive linear model of the mask, one coefficient per module, so no pairwise
term exists to tell it that two units are costly together; and recovering usable coefficients takes on
the order of a thousand perturbation evaluations plus several rounds in which the priors are re-
estimated. Our M ablations are one per unit, and what they assemble is the pairwise matrix itself.
(iii) Recovery-based pipelines (Minitron (Muralidharan et al., 2024)) restore ability via distillation;
they are complementary and orthogonal to the no-recovery regime we study, and could be stacked
on top of CoCurve.

Rotation/low-rank residual pruning (SliceGPT). SliceGPT (Ashkboos et al., 2024) compresses
by rotating the residual stream into a variance-ordered basis (a computational-invariance transform)
and slicing its least-significant dimensions, a fundamentally different mechanism from removing
structured units. It is the canonical member of the rotation family and complements our unit-removal
baselines. Two of its three headline models improve with recovery fine-tuning, which the selection-
only protocol here sets aside, and a faithful no-recovery form of it requires the official per-layer
rotation with residual adapters; the single global rotation is a substantially weaker variant, and a
number produced from it would understate the method. We therefore position SliceGPT by mecha-
nism rather than by a number its authors would not recognise, and note that its residual-rotation idea
is orthogonal to cross-module co-pruning curvature and could in principle be composed with it.

Curvature-based structured pruning (LLM Surgeon). The closest method in spirit is LLM
Surgeon (van der Ouderaa et al., 2024), which also uses second-order (Kronecker-factored Fisher)
curvature for structured pruning. Two differences are decisive under our regime. (a) Locality: its
Fisher factors model correlations among the weights inside one layer, whereas H here is an inter-
action between structured units anywhere in the network. That difference is measured rather than
assumed: the attention↔FFN block is 0.74–0.98 times the mean of the two within-module blocks
on all five models, and coupling persists far from the diagonal band (Figure 3b–d), so a within-layer
factorisation discards mass of the same order as the mass it keeps. Table 3 then measures what the
off-diagonal is worth: the λ=0 arm loses on ten models, and the fixed-allocation control shows the

gain is not a re-split of the budget between module types. (b) Recovery: its defining component is a
closed-form correlated weight update that repairs the remaining weights after each removal, a form
of weight repair that our selection-only protocol disables for every method (as we do for SlimGPT’s
OBS update). Its repair step is orthogonal to our selection objective and could be stacked on top
of CoCurve’s selection, but it is the method, so we do not report a version of it with the method
removed. What its absence leaves untested is whether modelling interactions at all is what helps,
and that is tested twice without it: by the λ = 0 arm of Table 3, which loses on eight of ten models
and is returned unchanged on the other two, and by the block-mean arm of Table 21, which keeps
the cross-component block but collapses each block to its scalar mean — the interaction granularity
of SAViT — so that the interaction decides how much of each component type to remove and never
which unit. That arm, given its own calibrated λ̂, loses six of seven columns and all four capability
groups to the full matrix, so the benefit is not reproduced by a component-level budget.

Cross-structure joint importance in vision (SAViT). The closest precedent in spirit is
SAViT (Zheng et al., 2022), which first argued that pruning should account for interactions among
heterogeneous structural components rather than scoring them independently, realized through a
collaborative Taylor importance over ViT heads/channels/embeddings. Three differences are con-
crete and empirically anchored. (a) Domain/objective: SAViT targets vision Transformers with a
task-supervised Taylor importance; CoCurve derives its edges from a label-free token-level KL self-
distillation on text. (b) Training: SAViT realizes its pruning ratios through collaborative optimisa-
tion or fine-tuning, whereas CoCurve is strictly one-shot and selection-only, under the no-recovery
protocol of Section 3.1. (c) Mechanism: SAViT’s interaction is a global importance budget over
components; CoCurve forms the explicit attention↔FFN off-diagonal Fisher block and uses it di-
rectly as co-pruning saliency. Empirically, the value of that block over a no-interaction baseline is
exactly the diagonal-only (λ=0) row of Table 3: CoCurve recovers the cross-structure benefit SAViT
motivates, but with no optimisation or fine-tuning.

Concurrent “dual Taylor” sparsification (D2 Prune). The concurrent D2 Prune (Xiong et al.,
2026) shares the “Taylor” vocabulary but is mechanistically orthogonal, and the distinction lands on
a row already in our tables. Its “dual” expansion is over weights and activations for unstructured
and N :M reconstruction, and it discards the weight–activation cross block as higher-order negli-
gible, keeping an OBS saliency built on a dense inverse Hessian with an attention-distribution KL
regulariser. The term it drops is on a different axis from ours, and the two are best read as shar-
ing a vocabulary rather than an object: the gap between the diagonal-only (λ=0) selector and full
CoCurve in Table 3 quantifies, at structured granularity under a global token-KL objective, exactly
what discarding the cross term forgoes. Moreover, Proposition 5 shows this omission is a second-
order mass scaled by the mean off-diagonal correlation, not the higher-order remainder D2 Prune
assumes it to be.

E.2   L IMITATIONS

Selection cost. Estimating H costs hours rather than the minutes an activation-norm criterion needs.
It is a one-off: the matrix depends on the model and the calibration set, not on the compression ratio
or on λ, so a single estimate serves every ratio and the entire regularisation path (Section D.4.1).
Selection only. This paper studies which units to remove. Weight reconstruction and fine-tuning are
orthogonal, and we hold them fixed, disabled for every method, so that the comparison isolates the
selection. Section D.3 reports what happens when they are enabled.
Operator coverage. Turning a mask into a smaller model requires a slicing path per attention and
FFN operator. We provide one for the architectures studied here and check every sliced model by its
realised parameter count (Section A.2); a new operator family needs a new path.
Dense attention stacks. We target dense Transformers whose layers all have attention heads and
an FFN, which is what makes a removable unit well defined. Two directions the field is moving in
break that assumption rather than the method. Under mixture-of-experts routing the units are already
sparsely activated, so what a removal costs depends on the router; and architectures that interleave
full attention with linear-recurrent or state-space layers raise the question one level earlier, since a
layer without heads has no unit of the kind U collects. Co-pruning curvature is defined by Eq. 1

What a pruned model is for. At the ratios studied here, training-free structured pruning does not

target size.

E.3   B ROADER I MPACT

Structured, training-free compression lowers the compute, memory, and energy cost of deploying
LLMs, broadening access and reducing inference footprint. Because CoCurve requires no labels or
fine-tuning, it does not introduce new task-specific data dependencies. As with any compression,
practitioners should re-evaluate safety-relevant behaviour on the compressed model, since pruning
shifts capabilities unevenly across tasks.
