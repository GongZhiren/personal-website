\section{Introduction}
On-device models\cite{chen2019deep_edge_computing} have emerged as a critical paradigm for personalized AI applications, particularly since the breakthrough of DeepSeek R1-32B \cite{guo2025deepseek}, which demonstrates that large language models (LLMs) can also achieve comparable performance to cloud-based LLMs(Openai-o1\cite{roumeliotis2023chatgpt}) while being deployed on local devices. 
However, on-device models introduce unique security risks. When personal models operate in white-box settings accessible to end-users, adversaries could potentially exploit the full model information to infer sensitive private data with minimal attack costs, such as \textbf{\textit{Model Stealing Attack(MSA)}}\cite{oliynyk2023stealing}. Therefore, a primary objective in security hardening of on-device models can be implemented by restricting adversaries access to model internals, which downgrades \textbf{\textit{white-box}} attacks to \textbf{\textit{black-box}} settings.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Styles/images/vanilla.pdf} % 图片文件名
    \caption{(A) Vanilla method: all adapters run in TEEs; (B) \method: selective TEE shielding of privacy-sensitive and compressed adapters}
    \label{fig:vanilla}
\end{figure}

However, protecting on-device LLMs are particularly challenging due to the \textbf{\textit{security-and-efficient trade-off}}.
For example, cryptographic methods such as Multi-Party Computation (MPC)\cite{zeng2023mpcvit}, and Homomorphic Encryption (HE)\cite{zimerman2024he} offer strict security guarantees in specific models, but these methods are often computationally expensive and degrading the accuracy of models, making them impractical for LLMs in real-world applications. Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs)(e.g., Intel SGX\cite{costan2016intel}), as hardware-isolated secure computing units, have been widely adopted in cloud computing scenarios for confidential computing tasks \cite{alibabatee,awsnitro}. However, its application to on-device LLMs faces fundamental limitations. Its implementations on edge devices are constrained by limited memory provided and lack the support of GPUs\cite{zhou2023nnsplitter}, which is unable to deploy the full model into TEEs and also has significant model inference latency \cite{tramer2018slalom}. 

Recent studies on secure machine learning inference leverage TEEs as the root of trust, enabling outsource of computationally intensive tasks to external, untrusted accelerators such as GPUs~\cite{shen2022soter,tramer2018slalom,zhang2024no,AnonymousCoder2025Phantom,sun2023shadownet,zhou2023nnsplitter}. 
These works typically delegate the majority of computations to GPUs, while placing critical operations within the TEE to ensure data confidentiality and integrity. These approaches can be broadly categorized into two groups: one partitions models at the layer level, selectively executing certain layers within TEEs; the other adopts obfuscated execution paths, where multiple computational paths are generated during inference, yet only one path computes the actual result. It is worth noting, however, that these existing studies predominantly target relatively small model architectures (e.g., ResNet\cite{he2016deep_resnet}, VGG\cite{simonyan2014vgg}) and have not yet considered LLMs. Furthermore, current solutions incur significant communication overhead between TEEs and GPUs, largely due to the protection of non-linear operations within TEEs to securely outsource computations. Thus, such methodologies may not be practical in recent LLM parameter efficient fine tuning(PEFT) scenarios, where the objective is to outsource inference computations of publicly available model weights, while protecting privately trained model weights in TEEs.

In this paper, we propose leveraging Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) \cite{hu2022lora} to handle the memory and computational constraints instead of deploying full models within TEEs, which achieves competitive performance compared to full model fine-tuning with significantly fewer parameters\cite{zhu2024melo}. Since only LoRA parameters are trainable, the vanilla pattern deploys all LoRA adapter within the TEE to ensure privacy as presented in Figure \ref{fig:vanilla}(A), while the backbone LLM remains frozen to prevent potential leakage of private information from local data.

However, the latency breakdown in Figure \ref{fig:vanilla}(A) reveals a significant inference overhead compared to GPU-only execution. The \textbf{primary bottleneck} stems from \textbf{frequent data transfers} between the TEE and GPU, accounting for \textbf{41\%} of total inference time. And the \textbf{second bottleneck} is accounting for the computation in TEEs, which is almost \textbf{2× slower than GPU} execution. Therefore, above issues arise our \textit{\textbf{First Challenge: how to minimizing data transfer and computation costs while preserving the privacy of adapters.}} We formulate it as an adapter allocation problem: placing privacy-sensitive adapters in TEEs while offloading non-sensitive ones to GPUs. This hybrid approach minimizes both data transfer and computation costs while ensuring less privacy leakage through external adapters. Nevertheless, this design presents a \textbf{\textit{Second Challenge: how to effectively evaluate the privacy of LoRA adapters while ensuring that externally trainable adapters do not compromise sensitive information.}}

In this paper, we propose an end-to-end TEE-shielded LLM privacy-preserving framework, as presented in Figure \ref{fig:vanilla} (B), where only partially more privacy-sensitive and compressed adapters are placed in TEEs. This hybrid framework has \textbf{2.14$\times$ speed up with same security guarantee} against vanilla under MSA. \method focus on training phase to select privacy-sensitive layers as well as compressing its adapters' parameters. First, we present a \textbf{\textit{Reinforcement Learning(RL) Layer-Wise Privacy Awareness}} Method to figure out which layers are more privacy-sensitive to local data, and the sensitivity of layers is evaluated through \textbf{\textit{Gaussian Noise}}\cite{adamczewski2023differential_privacy}, which is integrated into RL's update policy. Second, we propose \textbf{\textit{Dynamic Shielded Adapter Compression}} to reduce computational overhead in TEEs. Unlike conventional methods that prune all parameters in each inserted adapter of the full model, we dynamically prune only the higher privacy-sensitivity adapters during fine-tuning. This approach significantly mitigates the accuracy degradation typically associated with pruning. Additionally, \method consists of two phases: Reinforcement Learning and Fine Tuning, which are executed alternately in parallel. This strategy can greatly reduce training costs against previous works\cite{zhang2024no,AnonymousCoder2025Phantom}, which require significant training time (e.g., Phantom takes 38 hours). Afterwards, when we get the privacy score of layers, we deploy adapters with higher privacy-sensitive to TEEs, and the less sensitive adapters as well as public weights are in GPUs. Meanwhile, even outside adapters contain less private information, we still inject noise into them to better defend model attacks, as this noise has minimal impact on model accuracy. In summary, our \textbf{\textit{contributions}} are threefold:

%% 需要重写contribution
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \item We propose a novel privacy-preserving framework to secure the parameter efficient fine tuned LLMs on device, which is much more efficient than previous works both in training and inference phase.
    \item We design a RL strategy combined with Gaussian Noise to evaluate the sensitivity of trainable layers, then deploy higher sensitivity layers into TEEs. Meanwhile, we propose the Dynamic Model Compression Strategy that can further reduce the computation cost.
    \item Comprehensive experiments show that our proposed method outperform the state-of-the-are methods.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background \& Related Works}
\label{related_works}
\subsection{TEE-Shielded Model Protection}
Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) are hardware-enforced isolated enclaves designed to secure sensitive data and computations against adversarial access, such as ARM TrustZone~\cite{alves2004trustzone}, Intel SGX~\cite{costan2016intel}. However, memory constraints (e.g., SGX’s limited Processor on device Reserved Memory of up to 128 MB\cite{zhou2023nnsplitter}) significantly hinder deploying large models on edge device. 
TEE-Shielded Model Protection framework aims to safeguard partial critical components of deep neural network(DNN) models using TEEs to balance deployment efficiency, effectiveness, and security. Such methods downgrade while-box to black-box attacks by shielding critical model computations, raising the barrier for adversaries into label-only black-box attacks. 

Recent works\cite{AnonymousCoder2025Phantom,zhou2023nnsplitter,islam2023confidential,zhang2024no,sun2023shadownet,mo2020darknetz} have explored hardware-assisted methods for securing DNN inference. Fo instance, TEESlice\cite{zhang2024no} propose a dynamic slicing mechanism for ResNet-34/VGG16, where extra slices are introduced and those with minimal accuracy loss slices are dropped, only critical slices are placed in TEEs for privacy preserving. Phantom is the most recent related work that introduce reinforcement learning-optimized obfuscation layers that degrade model performance for unauthorized users while maintaining functionality for authorized executions. 
Although these methods provide strong security guarantees for DNNs, they are neither designed for nor directly applicable to Large Language Models (LLMs). Moreover, they suffer from data transfer and TEE-computation overhead, which fundamentally limits their deployment in on-device LLM scenarios.

\subsection{LLM Compression} 
The discovery of sparse sub-networks in Transformer models has been validated by recent studies\cite{ma2023llm_prune,zhang2023loraprune,lv2023lightformer,hoefler2021sparsity_prune}. Previous works have found that models can be greatly compressed(almost over 50\% parameters) with very little drop in accuracy. However, conventional methods like Singular Value Decomposition(SVD) compression\cite{wang2024svd} or Quantization\cite{radford2023robust}, while effective in compressing models, have been found to be difficult to use in TEEs, which do not support its implementation optimization. Thus, we employ structural model pruning, which is more suitable for storing sparse matrices in TEEs. However, conventional pruning methods\cite{zhang2023loraprune} cannot be applied in our dynamic training scenario that requires layer-wise pruning. To address this, we propose a Dynamic Shielded Adapter Compression method specifically designed for TEEs focusing on partial privacy-sensitive adapters.

\section{Threats Model}
\label{Threats}
In this study, we set the security baseline as TEE protects the trained parameters of the LLM and only returns the predicted labels to external queries, following recent works\cite{zhang2024no,AnonymousCoder2025Phantom}. Moreover, informed by works\cite{sun2023shadownet}, we assume the attacker can observe and steal the model knowledge and data outside the TEE, but can not access the weight inside the TEEs. And the attacker can also access to the open-sourced LLMs\cite{touvron2023llama,zhang2022opt} to improve attack accuracy. We assume that the attacker can only query the victim model a limited number of times (less than 1\% of the training data) to build a transfer dataset, and used to train a surrogate model for MSA, which aligns with realistic scenarios where model queries are often assumed on prior works \cite{zhang2024no,oliynyk2023know}.

\section{Main Methodology}
\label{method}
\subsection{Overview}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Styles/images/framework_v2.pdf} % 图片文件名
    \caption{The Training Phase of Privacy-sensitive Layer Selection \& Compression}
    \label{fig:mainmethods}
\end{figure}
In this study, we propose a new training framework as shown in Fig.\ref{fig:mainmethods}, which comprises two core modules: \textbf{\textit{RL-Layer Wise Privacy Awareness(RL-Phase)}} and \textbf{\textit{Dynamic Shielded Adapter Compression(DSAC)}}. In addition, Parameter Efficient Model Fine Tuning(FT-Phase) is used to train the model. RL-Phase and FT-Phase are trained in alternate manner~(e.g., RL executed after each 10 steps of FT). Specifically, RL-Layer Wise Privacy Awareness consists of three stages: \textbf{(1)} Sampling candidate privacy sensitive layers based on updated privacy scores for next step of RL; \textbf{(2)} Privacy-Sensitive Evaluation for each layer independently by injecting gaussian noise into trainable weights; \textbf{(3)} Computing evaluated layers' rewards according to the loss feedback and updating layer privacy scores(environment states). Adapter Compression is executed during FT-Phase in fixed steps(e.g., in each 100 steps). Its pruning primarily focus on higher privacy-sensitive layers dynamically.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
The target \textbf{F} of this framework is aiming to satisfy: \textbf{(S) Security}, \textbf{(E) Efficiency}, \textbf{(P) Performance} for LoRA based on-device LLMs. Thus, the objective can be formulated as: 
\begin{equation}
    F = 
    \begin{cases}
        \underset{\Delta W}{\arg\min}\ \mathcal{L}_\text{FT}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}_\tau); \Delta W) + \underset{|\Delta W|}{\arg\min}\ \mathcal{T}_\mathcal{DSAC}(\Delta W;\vec{\mathcal{S}}), & \text{In FT-Phase} \\[8pt]
        \underset{\vec{\mathcal{S}}}{\arg\max}\ \mathcal{J}_\text{RL}(\vec{\mathcal{S}}|\Delta W^{*},\vec{\mathcal{A}}), & \text{In RL-Phase}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Given model $\mathcal{M}$, adapter parameters $\Delta W = BA$, and local train data~$\mathcal{D}_\tau$, test data~$\mathcal{D}_v$, the $\mathcal{L}_\text{FT}$ aims to optimize the performance of model, accompanying with dynamic model pruning for minimizing the number of adapter parameters $|\Delta W|$ based on gradients of $\Delta W$ and privacy score $\vec{\mathcal{S}} = [s_1,s_2,...,s_n]$ of $n$ layers. $\mathcal{J}_\text{RL}$ intend to quantify and maximize the privacy score $\mathcal{S}$ of layers for minimizing the shielded adapters in TEEs. Its updating is based on evaluation of noise $\epsilon$ injected adapters and the Policy Actions $\vec{\mathcal{A}} = [a_1,a_2,...,a_t ]$ in RL steps. During inference, the simplified hybrid architecture has presented in Fig.\ref{fig:vanilla}, where partial $K$ privacy-sensitive adapters are deployed in TEEs, while others are outsourcing to GPUs for acceleration.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning based Layer-Wise Privacy Awareness}
%% 提取这部分的工作的重点 可能需要在开头说明一下?
In this section, we describe the details of the RL-Phase for learning the layer-wise privacy scores $\mathcal{S}$. First, we model this stage as a contextual multi-armed bandit problem~\cite{arulkumaran2017deep}, where the RL-Phase learns to select candidate layers for TEE protection based on continuously updated privacy scores. During each round(also means the step of RL) of FT training, we set only the selected candidate layers as learnable (regard as an action $a_i$) in order to evaluate their privacy sensitivity.
% Inspired by the concept of Stochastic Policy Optimization (SPO) \cite{schulman2017proximal}, we employ a policy-based RL approach to optimize the privacy scores of decoder layers, in order to achieve an optimal trade-off between security and performance. 
The objective of RL Phase is $\mathcal{J}_\text{RL}(\mathcal{S}|\Delta W^{*},\vec{\mathcal{A}})$, which aims to minimize the number of shielded adapters in TEEs while keep the security of entire model. The entire learning flow is $(S_0,a_0,S_1,a_1,...)$, which denotes the action chosen based on its privacy score step by step. 
%% 在appendics中添加收敛性证明

\paragraph{\ding{172} Policy Action -- Candidate Privacy Sensitive Layer Selection:}
Start from model $\mathcal{M}$ with adapters, the privacy score $\mathcal{S}$ of layers are regarded as environmental state, which is core element in RL, and initialized with $\mathbf{S}^{(0)} = \mathbf{0}$. This dynamic score updating mechanism governs the policy action decisions. The policy action space $A = [a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_t]$ constitutes the set of decisions made during RL Phase on selecting of candidate privacy layers in each step. To figure out the privacy distribution across layers, we utilize a weighted uniform distribution combined with privacy scores $\mathcal{S}$ to sampling candidate layers that would be trained during FT Phase. Therefore, the policy action $\pi_i$ is formally defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
\pi_i \sim U\bigl(0, sigmoid(s_i)\bigr)
\label{eq:pi_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $U$ represents a uniform random sampling over the interval $[0, \sigma(s_i)]$. At each RL-step, a probability $p_i$ is randomly sampled from $\pi_i$, resulting in a probability set $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n\}$ for all layers that would be chosen for next FT-Phase. Concretely, we select $N_t$ layers from the model based on the probability set $\mathcal{P}$, which identifies higher privacy sensitivity. After FT training, noise is injected into the adapter weights of these $N_t$ layers to quantify privacy. However, due to the inherent depth of large models and the inaccuracy of the action policy during the initial training stages, random sampling may cause a large number of adjacent layers selected and lead to training instability. Moreover, consolidating all high-privacy layers into contiguous segments may compromise both the model's security and performance. Therefore, we partition the total layers into $G = \{g_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ groups and sample $\frac{N_t}{G}$ layers from each group. 
%% 给出一个最后的输出
Afterwards, the candidate model for next FT training is:
\begin{equation}
    l_i = 
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if } i \in \theta \\[4pt]
        0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}, \quad \text{where} \quad \theta = \{\, i \mid p_i > p_{g_{\tau}} \,\}
    \label{eq:ahp_definition}
\end{equation}
where $G$ is empirically set to 3 by default. $\theta$ is the selection strategy that if $p_i$ larger than threshold $p_{g_{\tau}}$ in corresponding group. The i-th layer $l_i$ will be chosen as learnable layers in subsequent step and evaluating its privacy sensitivity.

\paragraph{\ding{173}Privacy-Sensitive Evaluation:} 
Layer Wise Privacy-Sensitivity is qualified through \textit{differential privacy (DP)-inspired noise injection}. 
For each layer, we inject gaussian noise to adapter low-rank matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{rank}}\times d_{\text{out}}}$ independently. Then, we conduct forward computations to candidate noise injected models to evaluate their privacy sensitivity by measuring the loss degradation. This idea is motivated by the observation that higher sensitivity to parameter perturbations correlates with private data patterns\cite{zhelnin2024gift}. %% 在appendics加入不同数据集对噪声敏感度的分析 
Furthermore, the setting of noise is rely on the weights of adapters, which then can guarantee the noise only destroy the impact of adapters' weights, while still keep the impact of base model's weights.

As showed in Fig.~\ref{fig:variance_compare}, the weight of base model ViT $\mathcal{M}$ has relatively larger variance than fine-tuned adapters. Thus, the noise has a negligible impact on base model weights. Meanwhile, as the weights' variance is various towards the depth of layers, we consider using flexible $\sigma$ to mitigate the bias of noise on specific layers' weights, such adaptive noise strategy is also adopted in DP-gradients\cite{adamczewski2023differential_privacy,lee2018concentrated_privacy_budget}.
% Formally, the forward propagation of layers is $W = xW + x(A \cdot B), \text{where}, \|A\| \ll \|W\|$, and $Output = X + MHA(X)$.  Thus, even if noise has large impact on multi-head attention in this layer, it still has residual connection to mitigate the influence.
Formally, the noise injection of adapter in specific layer is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{noise_injection}
f = xW + xB \cdot A \longrightarrow f = xW + x(A + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)) \cdot B,\ \ \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_i^2)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{\ding{174} Reward Feedback -- Layer-wise Privacy Score Updates:}
The update of privacy score is based on rewards feedback of policy action $a_i$. The reward will be computed according to the change in loss before and after injecting noise into the model adapter weights in eq.\ref{noise_injection}. Following is the i-th layers' change of loss defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \left[\mathcal{L}(x_b;l_i') -\mathcal{L}(x_b;l_i)\right]
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the batch size of validation data~$\mathcal{D}_v$. $l_i'$ denotes noise injection of layer's adapter. 
Following is the computation of the reward according to the change of loss formulating as: 
% \begin{equation}
%       R_i^t = \begin{cases}
%           e^{\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t} - \frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}e^{\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t} & \Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t \ge 0 \\
%           -e^{\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t} + \frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}e^{\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t} & \Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t \le 0
%       \end{cases}  
% \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    R_i^t = \mathrm{sgn}(\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t)\left(e^{\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t} - \frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}e^{\Delta\mathcal{L}_j^t}\right)
\end{equation}
where $R_i^t$ denotes the reward for $i$-th layer at step $t$. And $sgn(\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t)$ is regarded as privacy signals, that if $\Delta\mathcal{L}_i^t$ is greater than zero, the layer is considered to exhibit higher privacy-sensitivity to Gaussian noise, and vice versa. Consequently, if the layer identified as having higher privacy-sensitivity will be rewarded, whereas the privacy score of low-privacy layers will be penalized.

Moreover, since the interval-based interactive training strategy is used during the training between the RL-Phase and the FT-Phase, we only update trained candidate layers in this RL-step to prevent continuous degradation of none-trained layers, which would cause loss collapse empirically. Specifically, the update rule for the privacy score is formulated as follows:
\begin{equation}
s_i = s_i + r_i^t \cdot \mu \cdot sigmoid(s_i) \cdot (1 - sigmoid(s_i)), \quad \text{if  } l_i = 1
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is learning rate of RL, controlling the convergence of privacy scores.
Afterward,  when identifying privacy-sensitive layers, these layers will be ranked and deployed in TEEs to satisfy semi-blackbox setting which has similar security guarantee as full blackbox setting.

\subsection{Dynamic Shielded Adapter Compression}
Besides the frequent data transfer between TEEs and GPUs, computation costs are also big bottleneck of TEEs due to the absence of GPU support. Therefore, we consider to employ structural weights pruning for \textbf{compressing shielded adapters and feature dimensions}. It can \textbf{address the computation issue}, while feature dimensions reduction can also \textbf{mitigate the communication bandwidth limitations} of TEEs.
In the following, we will briefly introduce the gradient guided weight pruning, and describe how we use it to dynamically prune the higher privacy layers' adapter weights. 

\paragraph{LoRA Weights Importance Estimation:}
Thanks to previous works \cite{zhang2023loraprune,ma2023llm_prune,molchanov2019pruning}, the importance of weight element $W_{i,j} \in W$ can be quantified by measuring the impact of its removal on loss. For an input $x$ and the ground-truth prediction $y$, the induced error of $W_{i,j}$ can be given as:
\begin{equation}
    I_{i,j} = \left[ L(x,y,W+(BA)) - L(x,y,W+(BA) | W_{i,j} = 0) \right]^2
\end{equation}

Computing $I_{i,j}$ for each weight is computationally expensive. Thus, we can use First-order Taylor Expansion to approximate the importance $\hat{I}_{i,j}$ by setting the element $(BA)_{i,j} = -W_{i,j}$ if the element $W_{i,j} \in W_0$ is removed. Then the importance of weights in LoRA can be formulated in:
\begin{equation}
       \hat{I}_{i,j} = [(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial B_{i,:}}A_{:,j}+B_{i,:}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{A_{:,j}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial B_{i,:}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial A_{:,j}})(W_{i,j}+(BA)_{i,j})]^2
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Shielded Adapter Pruning:}
In the initial phase of model training, layers exhibits relatively lower and random sensitivity to private data, resulting in an unclear distribution of privacy layers, which is difficult to determine the candidate layers that should be pruned. Moreover, estimating weight importance and pruning with limited training data during this phase is prone to inaccuracies, which can empirically lead to significant bias and performance degradation. Thus, we propose a \textbf{dynamic pruning strategy} specifically adapted for RL-compatible pruning. This design ensures both structural pruning compatibility and training stability.

Formally, we propose the dynamic pruning ratio for adapters in $i$-th layer as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ratio}_i(t) = \frac{R_{\text{total}} - R_{\text{cur}}(t_{ft})}{(T_{ft} - t_{ft} + \delta) \cdot sigmoid(s_i)} \cdot \left(1 -\alpha + \alpha \cdot \frac{t_{ft}}{T_{ft}}\right)
\end{equation}
where $T_{ft}$ is total FT steps, $t_{ft}$ is current FT steps, $R_{total}$ is overall prune ratio, and $R_{cur}$ is cumulative prune ratio. In addition, $\alpha$ is hyper-parameter to control prune rate, so as to the on-step pruning ratio will progressively increase in a linear manner. This design satisfies the configuration where pruning is more likely to be performed during the later training stages.

Specifically, we adopt weight mask to implement structural pruning on heads. The head importance $H_i$ is computed by averaging importance of weight elements in this head, and we also applied moving average that $H_i^t = \beta H_{i}^{t} + (1-\beta)H_i^{t-1}$ to mitigate bias caused by single batch of data.
Thereby, weight mask is defined by ranking the head importance, where $N_{head}$ is total number of heads:
\begin{equation}
       \text{Mask} = \begin{cases} 
   0 & \text{if } H_i \in \text{Rank}\left(\vec{H}; \lfloor \text{ratio}(t) \cdot N_{head} \rfloor\right) \\
   1 & \text{otherwise}
   \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Afterwards, the forward process of adapter with mask can be formulated as :
\begin{equation}
    f = xW + xBA \odot \text{Mask}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes Hardamard product. To be note, we do not prune parameters in base model to prevent performance decay. This module addressed the issue of computation costs by compressing the parameters in TEEs as well as reducing feature maps during each data transfer.

The complete algorithm is presented in Appendix\ref{}.

\subsection{Performance \& Privacy Enhancement}
\paragraph{Fast Recovery for Secure Layers}
Although we only prune weights with lower importance, the model still experiences slight performance degradation empirically. To address this, we adopt a post-training strategy with a fast recovery process to enhance the model performance within a limited number of epochs. Notably, during the post-training phase, only layers intended to deployed in TEEs are fine-tuned, thereby preventing potential privacy leakage of sensitive data.

\paragraph{Noise inject to layers in GPU}
We use Gaussian noise to estimate the privacy score of each layer in LLMs by comparing the loss change before and after noise injection. Specifically, layers whose loss decreases after noise injection are penalized, as this indicates lower sensitivity to noise. Conversely, layers that are non-sensitive to noise can still benefit from noise injection during inference on GPUs, as the noise does not degrade their performance and can enhance defense against model inference attacks. Therefore, noise is selectively injected into the weights of lower-privacy layers to achieve this balance.

\section{Experiments}
To demonstrate the Security, Efficiency, and Performance of our framework, we conduct experiments primarily from three aspects:\\
\textit{(a).Whether \method achieves \textbf{superior efficiency} in on-device reasoning?}\\
\textit{(b).Whether \method can \textbf{guarantee the privacy} of local data like blackbox setting?}\\
\textit{(c).Whether each module has \textbf{no accuracy degradation} on models to the vanilla setting?}

\subsection{Models and Datasets} We evaluate the effectiveness of \method on pre-trained large models including language models(OPT-2.7B~\cite{zhang2022opt}, Llama-7B~\cite{touvron2023llama}) and Vision Transformer (ViT-B-12, ViT-Large-14\cite{zeng2023mpcvit}) for vision tasks. We adopt LoRA\cite{hu2022lora} as Adapter for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Specifically, the fine tuning of NLP domain use dataset (CommonSenseQA)\cite{hu2023llm_dataset} and validated by six widely-used question-answering benchmarks: ARC-Challenge and ARC-Easy\cite{clark2018think_arc}, HellaSwag~\cite{zellers2019hellaswag}, OBQA~\cite{mihaylov2018obqa}, PIQA~\cite{bisk2020piqa}, and WinoGrande~\cite{sakaguchi2021winogrande}. For the vision experiments, we employ six classical benchmark datasets: CIFAR~\cite{cifar}, UTKFace~\cite{zhang2017age_utkface}, MNIST~\cite{cohen2017emnist}, GTSRB~\cite{stallkamp2012man_gtsrb}, and SUN397~\cite{xiao2010sun_397}. Note that CIFAR and UTKFace datasets are also utilized to evaluate the security guarantee of \method under Model Stealing Attacks (MSA), following previous baseline works~\cite{zhou2023nnsplitter, AnonymousCoder2025Phantom}.

\subsection{Implementation and Evaluation Metrics} 
All training phases are conducted in PyTorch 2.5.1 using four NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. For inference mode, we build a prototype framework on a PC equipped with an Intel SGX enclaves(SDK 2.6, GCC 7.5), and NVIDIA RTX4090D 24GB GPU to evaluate the runtime speed-up of \method on real devices, following the setting from TEESlice~\cite{zhang2024no}. More details(such as attack pipeline, hyperparameters, and etc) of implementations are provided in appendicx\ref{}.

For fair comparison, we use the \textit{lm-eval-harness}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness}} as NLP evaluation framework. For Vision tasks, we evaluate the performance following settings from previous works~\cite{hu2022lora, AnonymousCoder2025Phantom}. To assess security and efficiency, we adopt two most recent works TEESlice~\cite{zhang2024no} and Phantom~\cite{AnonymousCoder2025Phantom} as baselines with consistent evaluate metrics.
